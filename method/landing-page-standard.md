# Landing Page Standard — craft best practice for venture launch assets

**Owner:** Head of Product · **Capability:** Forge WS1 — CF development
**Added:** 27 August 2026, on Tom's instruction, after applying it to the Calmly Resolve homepage.
**Parent:** `derivation-standard.md` · **Content standard:** `sales-marketing-asset-standard.md`
**Source:** Julian Shapiro, *Landing Pages* — https://www.julian.com/guide/startup/landing-pages

**What this is, and is not.** This is a **craft standard for an output the CF produces** — the launch assets of an architected venture — not a change to the CF method. It does not touch Simanis, the BALM disciplines or the criteria system, so the strict folding rule does not bite. It sits alongside `llm-best-practice-scan.md` (which watches LLM practice) as the equivalent watch for venture-asset craft.

## 1 · The formula everything else serves

> **Purchase rate = Desire − (Labor + Confusion)**

Three levers, and only three. Amplify desire; reduce the work asked of the visitor; remove ambiguity. Every element on the page is justified by one of them or it comes off.

## 2 · The structure — seven elements, in order

1. **Navbar** — logo, a few links
2. **Hero** — header, subheader, imagery
3. **Social proof** — press or customer logos
4. **CTA**
5. **Features and objections** — 3 to 6
6. **Repeat CTA**
7. **Footer**

**Do not depart from the sequence without a stated reason.** Deviation costs comprehension; visitors are pattern-matching, and an unfamiliar order reads as friction rather than originality.

## 3 · The header test

> *If the visitor reads only this text, will they know exactly what you sell?*

Fails: "Improve your workflow." "Supercharge collaboration." Passes: "Visually design and develop sites from scratch. No coding." "Groceries delivered in 1 hour."

Build it in two moves:

**Move 1 — the value proposition.** What inadequate alternative do they use now? How is this better? Turn that into an action statement.

**Move 2 — the hook.** Either a **bold claim** (specific, surprising — "a dopamine hit of *I didn't know that was possible*") or an **objection addressed up front** (find it by asking customers "what almost stopped you buying?" and non-customers "why haven't you?").

**Subheader:** one or two sentences, carrying *mechanism* (how it works) and *credibility* (why the bold claim is believable).

## 4 · Features — three components each

- **Header** — a value prop in direct language. Not "empower your sales." State the benefit so a reader can decide in a second whether it is relevant to them.
- **Paragraph** — describe it, and answer the objection that would stop the purchase.
- **Image** — product screenshots or GIFs showing the thing working. **Not abstract imagery.** For physical products, several use cases plus close-ups.

Features must tie back to the hero's dominant claim. The page is one argument, not a list.

## 5 · CTA

A continuation of the hero's narrative, in the same verbs. "Find food." "Start learning." Not "Request a meeting."

## 6 · The six-question review

Test on two reader types — **outside the market** (is it clear to someone who doesn't know the space?) and **inside it** (is it differentiated?). Ask both:

1. **Conversion** — would you sign up now? What is missing?
2. **Interest** — 1–10. What needs rewriting?
3. **Clarity** — what is unexplained?
4. **Expansion** — what deserves more depth?
5. **Brevity** — what is dispensable?
6. **Disbelief** — what made you sceptical?

## 7 · What applying it to Calmly taught us — the part worth keeping

Two findings surfaced by running the standard against a real venture page. Both generalise.

### 7.1 · A vocabulary register and landing-page clarity pull against each other — and the register must be scoped, not abandoned

Ventures with legal or regulatory exposure develop an operating vocabulary: words that are exact, defensible, and meaningless to a cold reader. Calmly's register mandates *guaranteed exit* and *standing bid* and forbids *protection*, *compensation*, *settlement*. The header test demands a reader know what you sell from one line — and the permitted words do not deliver that alone.

**The resolution is to scope the register rather than relax it.** It governs the words that constitute the *promise* — what is offered, by whom, on what terms — because those carry the legal characterisation. It does not govern descriptive framing around the promise. So a page may say "your buyers hedge, and that costs you volume" (description) leading into "a guaranteed exit on every transaction" (the promise, in register).

**Generalises to:** any venture whose product words are constrained by regulation, standards bodies or a professional register. Name which words carry the characterisation, and let the rest be plain.

### 7.2 · Lead with the outcome the customer cannot get elsewhere — not the one that is easiest to quantify

Calmly's first draft led with **cost displacement** — dispute handling moving off the platform's book. It was wrong, and the venture's own record said so before the page was written.

The site-side value has two components: **cost displacement**, which the customer's existing team can already address imperfectly, and **foregone revenue**, which "the site's current team cannot address at all — it requires a credible guarantee that structurally changes participant behaviour." Only the second is exclusive to the architecture. And the arithmetic made it worse: for a platform not already running a guarantee scheme, displaced cost does not cover the fee at all, so the page was leading with a claim that fails on the customer's own numbers.

**The trap is that the weak half is the quantifiable half.** Cost displacement can be computed from a customer's inputs in a calculator; revenue uplift cannot, because nobody has measured it on their platform. The easiest thing to put a number on is not the thing worth leading with.

**The rule: lead with the component the architecture makes exclusive, and be explicit that the quantified tool prices the other one.** Calmly's calculator now says so on the page — it prices the cost you stop carrying, deliberately not the revenue you gain, because that has not been measured on your platform.

**The demotion only holds if the caveat travels with the calculator.** Any quantified tool on the page prices the computable half, so a page that demotes cost in its copy but leaves an uncaveated calculator quietly reverts to selling the computable half — the tool outranks the prose. State the limit at the tool, not only in the narrative. *(Cowork pass, 27 Aug 2026.)*

**A second trap, found by checking a live page against its source: evidence can support an adjacent mechanism rather than yours.** The Calmly page cited a marketplace study for a buyer-hesitation claim; the paper identifies a seller-side mechanism — quality rising, poor sellers exiting. The number was right and the causal story was not. **Cite what the source measures, then state your own claim separately and say it is yours.** A reader who opens the paper is the reader you most need to keep. *(27 Aug 2026.)*

**Generalises to:** every IVE venture, because the same split is structural. R2 decomposes the customer's key monetizable cost, and the components differ in whether an incumbent can address them. **The landing page's dominant claim should be the component that fails the incumbent's capability, not the one that computes most cleanly.** That is a direct line from the architecture to the copy, and it is checkable: the hero claim should trace to an R2 component the record says is exclusive.

### 7.3 · Pre-launch, "social proof" has no logos — say so

Element 3 assumes customers or press. A pre-launch venture has neither, and the guide's workaround (give the product to employees at recognisable companies) does not apply to B2B infrastructure with a four-signature dependency chain.

**What goes in the slot instead:** the evidence the claim rests on, labelled as what it is. For Calmly that is causally-identified published research on the mechanism, cited with the source, plus the state of independent review — and one line saying we are pre-launch and have no logos, which we would rather state than imply otherwise. Borrowed credibility that is honestly labelled beats a proof section that is quietly empty.

## 8 · Checklist before any venture landing page ships

- [ ] Header passes the descriptor test read cold, by someone outside the market
- [ ] Header carries a hook — bold claim or objection answered
- [ ] Subheader carries mechanism and credibility in ≤2 sentences
- [ ] Dominant claim traces to an R2 component the architecture record says is **exclusive** (§7.2)
- [ ] Register scoped: promise words exact, descriptive framing plain (§7.1)
- [ ] Proof slot filled honestly — logos, or labelled evidence, or a stated absence (§7.3)
- [ ] 3–6 features, each with header, objection-answering paragraph, and a **real product image**
- [ ] Features tie back to the hero claim
- [ ] CTA verbs continue the hero's narrative; CTA repeated
- [ ] Every quantified claim carries its band and source; nothing is invented to fill a slot
- [ ] Six-question review run with at least one reader outside the market
