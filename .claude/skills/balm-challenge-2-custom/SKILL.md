# BALM R2 — Eliminate Customers' Value Bottleneck

**Purpose:** Identify the single biggest monetizable cost in the target customer's current routines and produce an Efficacy Strategy that eliminates it. R2 sets two things simultaneously: the height of the customer value curve (what customers will pay) and the size of the addressable market (how many customers face this cost). Both together determine the market creation margin.

**When to run:** After R1 is productized. The Workaround Strategy from R1 gives the initial form factor that R2 works with — you are designing the value curve around an architecture that already has an R1 solution. Starting R2 without a productized R1 produces a customer value proposition designed around what customers want, not what can profitably be delivered.

**Output:** High-Import Outcome, Attendant Routines map, Key Monetizable Cost, Efficacy Theory of Change, Efficacy Strategy. Together these constitute the R2 component of the new Business Form Factor.

**Handoff to:** R3 (Circumvent Scaling Cost Bottleneck) — which addresses the balance sheet (working capital) problem during scaling, distinct from R2's value curve work.

## IVE framework — where this skill fits

**Integrated Venture Engine (IVE)** is a structured process for building new Core Business Architectures. Source: Simanis, E. et al. (2021), Cornell SC Johnson College of Business.

**Three nested levels of commercial architecture:**

| Level | What it is |
|-------|-----------|
| **Core Business Architecture (CBA)** | The logic that sets both the cost curve and value curve for an industry. No single company owns it. |
| **Business Form Factor (BFF)** | The essential shape product and operations take given a CBA. The default way the product is made, sold, delivered, and paid for. |
| **Business Model** | A company's unique strategy for outcompeting others within a shared BFF. |

IVE intervenes at the CBA/BFF level. Business model thinking comes after.

**The full sequence:**

```
PCO — Prime Commercial Opportunity (scope-setter; runs before all requirements)
│
├── F1 — Neutralise the Value Barrier
│   ├── R1: Circumvent At-scale Cost Bottleneck       [P&L — sets the cost floor]
│   ├── R2: Eliminate Customers' Value Bottleneck      [value ceiling — what customers pay]
│   └── R3: Circumvent Scaling Cost Bottleneck         [balance sheet — working capital at scale]
│
├── F2 — Normalise the Customer Routine
│   ├── R4: Circumvent Customer Doubt about Value
│   ├── R5: Eliminate the Biggest Learning Disruption
│   ├── R6: Circumvent the Cash Flow Constraint
│   └── R7: Activate Gateway Partners
│
└── F3 — Lock In the Market Position
    ├── R8: Create a Switching Cost
    ├── R9: Manufacture a Resource Moat
    └── R10: Manufacture Replaceability of the Key Supplier Input
```

**→ This skill: R2 — Eliminate Customers' Value Bottleneck** (F1, second requirement; requires productized R1)

**Design principle — architect for at-scale, not for launch.** Every requirement is designed for the venture operating at the scale required to pay back all investment capital at a competitive rate of return. Early-stage operational constraints (team size, regulatory budget, portfolio volume) are sequencing problems, not architecture problems. Do not let launch-phase limitations constrain the design. A cost that seems large at 50 units is often trivial against the same cost at 50,000 units.

## R2 definition

> Eliminate the target customers' biggest cost — measured in money (expended or foregone), fear (of failing to achieve the outcome), or stress (in managing current routines) — from their current routines for achieving the outcome of greatest importance that can be measurably impacted.

R2's impact shows up in the price customers will pay and in the size of the addressable market. These two together determine the market creation margin — the gap between what the venture can charge and what the R1 cost floor requires.

**Design principle — architect for at-scale.** R2 is designed for the venture operating at the scale needed to pay back all investment capital. The Key Monetizable Cost, the Efficacy Strategy, and the addressable market estimate are all calculated at that scale — not at the launch cohort. Early-stage pilots test the mechanism; they do not define the architecture.

## This skill IS a design loop (read first)

Running R2 is not a five-input form filled once. It is one turn of the IVE design loop — **Diagnose → Theorize → Productize → Simulate** — and the loop only closes when Simulate clears. The point of the loop is *learning by reasoning*: to reason this design to failure on paper and pivot **here, in simulation — not after launch, in market.**

The four steps map onto this skill's existing structure:
- **Diagnose** — High-Import Outcome + Attendant Routines map + Key Monetizable Cost (the customer value bottleneck this challenge isolates).
- **Theorize** — the Efficacy Theory of Change (named mechanism; prescriptive, not descriptive).
- **Productize** — the Efficacy Strategy + cumulative BFF (C1–C2) + the CTM/AOM update (the design decision and its operational imprint).
- **Simulate** — run the Model Stack (CTM→AOM→ARM→Financial Simulation→required cost per unit→FMOS); a real gate that can fail and loop you back. At R2 this is the first *definitive* FMOS check — the KMC is the price ceiling.

### Simulate = run the Model Stack — not "estimate a floor"

Simulate is the IVE Model Stack carried through to a number. It is **not** a guessed cost floor or a top-down `£X ÷ volume`. That shortcut is the "high resolution, low fidelity" failure Simanis (2023, *Financial Simulations*) warns against — detailed numbers disconnected from the commercial logic (the solar-fridge team modelled a cheaper sales channel the product couldn't actually use). The chain:

**CTM → AOM → ARM → Financial Simulation → required cost per unit → FMOS.**
- **CTM** (demand side): the 4-D product components each customer state change requires.
- **AOM** (supply side): who does what, where, at what volume, to deliver them (Ops Map of product/information/money flows; LMU first).
- **ARM** (At-Scale Resourcing Model): the cost structure built **from first principles via resource drivers** — the step that makes this a simulation, not a spreadsheet. Three driver types combine to size every resource: **customer transaction drivers** (reachable market, penetration, share, product life, churn — tie cost to unit sales), **work drivers** (the activities each resource supports), **activity drivers** (time/quantity per granular activity). Output: the four cost layers **PVC · RC · SC · IC**, LMU-first.
- **Financial Simulation**: project the ARM across the volume ramp + the scaling/investment period, **discount for cost of capital**, reduce to the **required cost (and price) per unit**.

**Fidelity guardrail (hard):** every cost must trace to a resource the architecture requires → to a work/activity driver → to a customer-transaction driver tied to unit sales. A cost that can't be traced that way is low-fidelity and does not go in the model. Conversely, every money-flow *outflow* on the Ops Map (e.g. a guarantee payout, a refund, a fee) MUST appear — leaving one out is the most common fidelity failure (it is how Calmly's first floor omitted the guarantee payout).

**Simulate — the gate with teeth (mandatory; do not skip on illustrative data).** A gate that cannot fail is theatre. Even with placeholder figures, the Simulate step must be able to fail — or the loop never fires and R2 has not been designed, only described. The Section 8 FIT gate enforces this; the rules below govern it.

1. **Use the correct FMOS formula — cost denominator.** `FMOS = (WTP_low − Cost_high) / Cost_high` (Simanis, *Built to Hold*, p.7). Do **not** use `(Price − Cost) / Price` (gross-margin / price-denominator) — it is a different, incompatible metric and understates the figure. WTP_low = the R2 Key Monetizable Cost / price ceiling; Cost_high = the AOM high-point unit cost. **Bands — single source of truth is `Forge/WS1/criteria-registry.md`; do not redefine locally.** Two gate levels, do not conflate: *This challenge's Simulate gate (per-requirement early warning):* **PASS ≥ 25% · BORDERLINE 15–24% · FAIL < 15%** — the band this skill's FIT section uses. *The whole-architecture / Phase II gate (not this skill — runs once, after all ten):* **PASS ≥ 60% · BORDERLINE 25–59% · FAIL < 25%** (60% survives the 62% average engineering cost overrun, Flyvbjerg & Gardner 2023). Use the per-requirement band here; the 60% bar is the final convergence criterion checked by the whole-architecture pass. If these ever disagree with the registry, the registry wins.
2. **Name the load-bearing assumptions.** Which 2–3 inputs does the FMOS most depend on?
3. **Run the stress test.** "What is FMOS if the three biggest cost/value assumptions all resolve badly at once?" (Flyvbjerg: complex projects average a 62% cost overrun — a passing design survives it.)
4. **State the pivot trigger explicitly.** Write the threshold at which the gate flips to FAIL and the specific result that would force a pivot: *"Would FAIL if [assumption] crosses [value] — that is the pivot trigger."* The bar for a sound design is exactly this sentence existing.
5. **Illustrative pass ≠ convergence.** If figures are placeholders (red), the verdict is **"⚠ Provisional pass — pending [the data that would settle it]; would FAIL if [trigger]."** Never a clean PASS on unvalidated inputs. The loop converges only when Simulate clears on inputs that *could have failed it*.

**Loop-back and the pivot discipline.**
- **FAIL or BORDERLINE → return to Diagnose** (Section 2 — the KMC / value bottleneck), not to a tweak. Re-run the loop with the FIT diagnosis as the new brief: which lever is off — cost floor too high, or value ceiling too low — and what changes.
- **A pivot changes the theorem or the strategy, not the tactics.** If the core mechanism is unchanged, it is optimisation, not a pivot.
  - *Pivot when:* FMOS is negative/below-gate and cannot be fixed within the current design; a key assumption is disconfirmed and the theorem no longer holds.
  - *Do not pivot when:* results are ambiguous, or the strategy wasn't actually tested (flawed execution of the reasoning).
- **Iteration cap.** If three loop-backs do not converge, stop and flag — do not spin. Write a `FLAG FOR TOM` block stating why the challenge will not clear and what decision is needed.
- **Close criterion.** Mark R2 complete only when Simulate clears (real PASS) or a BORDERLINE is consciously accepted with its conditions recorded as critical assumptions. An unvalidated provisional pass closes the *design* but leaves the loop open pending data — record it that way.

## Why "benefits" are not enough

"Better quality / faster / more convenient" makes a routine better but does not eliminate cost or suffering. Benefits and gain points are deceiving: "faster / easier / better" has very low monetizability in a new market. This framing works once a market exists and you are competing for position within it. For new market creation, if you are not eliminating significant cost or suffering, you are not building a viable venture — you are competing for attention in a market that does not yet exist.

**The adoption threshold.** Greater surplus value (the gap between the cost eliminated and the price charged) drives faster adoption. A smaller gap drives slower adoption — or no adoption. Costs must be large enough that elimination produces a compelling surplus. If the cost is genuinely small, customers will not change routines regardless of how elegant the solution is.

**The "right mindset" diagnostic.** If it is easy to answer R2's questions from memory — from what you already know about customers — the cost you have identified is probably small. The key structural cost is usually invisible to customers because they have adapted around it. If you can name it without deep observation, you have probably named a visible, non-structural cost. Slow down and observe routines before claiming the KMC is confirmed.

The customer's willingness to pay is determined by the size of the loss they are escaping, not by the quality of the experience you are offering.

## Research Clusters — FIELDWORK ONLY. Skip by default.

⛔ **This whole section applies only when a person is going out to do infield research with real customers. It is OFF by default (ruled by Tom, 28 Aug 2026). Do not run it, do not produce a cluster matrix, and do not report clusters as a gap, unless the user has specifically asked for fieldwork planning.**

**Why it is off by default.** Clusters exist for one purpose: to stop fieldwork carrying silent bias, by making sure the researcher covers each systematically different routine variant rather than interviewing whichever customers were easiest to reach. **That purpose only exists when someone is actually going to interview people.** A desk-based design run has no interview sample to bias, so the device has nothing to do — and running it anyway manufactures a segmentation exercise the design does not need, burns session time, and invites the runner to treat clusters as market segments, which they are not.

**Turn it on when, and only when:** the user asks for fieldwork planning, a research plan, an interview guide, or a sampling frame — or says in substance "we are going to go and talk to customers."

**When it is off, say so once and move on.** One line in the R2 output — *"Research clusters not run: desk-based design, no fieldwork planned."* Do not carry it as an open item, an NCR, or a completeness gap. It is not missing; it is not applicable.

**Everything below is the fieldwork procedure.** It is unchanged and correct. It simply does not run unless the trigger above is met.

Before gathering any customer data, define research clusters — broad groupings within the Prime Commercial Opportunity (PCO) that likely have systematically different routines.

**Research clusters are not customer segments.** They are not defined by demographics or psychographics. They are defined by structural differences in how the problem manifests — differences that create noise in research data if not controlled for.

**Why this matters:** variables like urban/rural location, organisational size, and life stage create systematic variation in routines. Defining clusters before fieldwork ensures representativeness across the PCO with the least research effort.

**Place factors (structural, not demographic):**
- Urban / Peri-urban / Rural
- Infrastructure maturity (grid access, connectivity, transport)
- Regulatory environment (jurisdiction, licensing regime)

**Profile factors:**
- Organisation class: Micro / SME / Enterprise
- Life stage / household composition
- Relationship to the problem (primary actor vs affected party)

**Research Cluster Template — 3-factor matrix.** For each cluster, define it across three dimensions before fieldwork begins:

| Dimension | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Place factor** | Structural location or infrastructure variable | Urban / Rural; grid-connected / off-grid |
| **Profile factor** | Actor characteristic that changes routine structure | Micro enterprise / SME; sole trader / employed |
| **Focal routine** | The specific routine within the HIO most likely to differ across this cluster | "Access inputs" vs "sell produce" vs "manage cash" |

Name the cluster by its distinguishing combination: e.g. "Urban SME — cash management routine." A cluster defined only by demographics is a segment, not a cluster.

### ⛔ Target-customer selection rubric — run this explicitly; do not assert the customer

The target customer is **derived, not assumed.** R2's job is to determine *which* customer carries the Key Monetizable Cost worth targeting — by sizing, not intuition. Grounded in Simanis (*Built to Hold*): the **Key Value Bottleneck (KVB)** is *"the factor responsible for the customer pain with the **highest monetizability — customer dollar value**,"* framed as the job-to-be-done (Christensen JTBD).

Run the four steps, in order:

1. **Enumerate candidate customers** — the genuinely distinct customer TYPES within the PCO (primary actor vs affected party vs payer vs intermediary). List them all. Do **not** pre-pick one. ⚠ **Clusters are NOT candidates (corrected 25 Aug 2026, Tom):** the selection rubric chooses between customer *types* — different actors. Clusters within a type are a research-coverage device: they exist because different people inside the TAM reach the outcome through systematically different routines, and fieldwork must cover each routine variant or the Attendant Routines map carries silent bias. The venture serves as many people within the TAM as possible — you never "select" one cluster and drop the rest. Sizing clusters is still useful (it verifies each carries material KMC and passes the adoption threshold, and it tells you which routine variant to map FIRST), but the output is coverage priorities, not a market exclusion.
2. **Size each candidate's KMC by monetizable dollar value × population.** For each: estimate the dollar value of the trapped pain × the number of actors who bear it — the PCO Value Potential (`Group Size × Penetration × Importance`). Use the monetizability spectrum (money > fear > stress) when comparing.
3. **Screen** each on loss-to-price (>2×) and the adoption threshold ("enough suffering to compel a routine change?").
4. **SELECT the candidate that maximises monetizable dollar value × population.** That is the target customer + KMC. **Legibility to the researcher is a secondary tiebreaker only — never the primary key.** Choosing the easy-to-observe customer over the highest-value one is a known failure.

⛔ **If you are naming the target customer without having sized the alternatives' KMCs, stop — you have skipped the rubric.** (Logged 21 June 2026: R2 asserted "customer = X" by intuition, never enumerating or sizing candidates. The customer must fall out of the sizing, not precede it.)

The selected (highest-value) customer becomes the **focus cluster** that anchors the Attendant Routines map; other clusters are compared against it for systematic variation.

Define clusters before fieldwork begins. Ensure research covers each cluster — gaps become silent biases in the Attendant Routines map.

### ⛔ TAM-membership check — run on every cluster before sizing (added 25 Aug 2026)

Every proposed cluster must be validated against the venture's qualifying TAM definition BEFORE it enters the sizing rubric. Check each cluster against every TAM gate (claim band, exclusion rules, sector gates, reachability — whatever the venture's locked definition specifies). A cluster can fail structurally, not just numerically: e.g. an actor class large enough to industrialise the routine may be excluded by construction (Calmly precedent, 25 Aug 2026 — the enterprise-defendant cluster failed the pattern-of-conduct gate: any defendant big enough to run a claims team generates quasi-pattern claim flow, which the TAM excludes; the cluster was struck before sizing). Record struck clusters with the gate that excluded them — do not silently drop them. Sizing a cluster that is outside the TAM inflates the addressable value and corrupts the target-customer selection.

### ⛔ TAM PARTITION RULE — segment by KMC, never by who-sues-whom (added 27 Aug 2026, Tom's ruling)

**A segment is a set of customers who share ONE key monetizable cost. If two sub-classes have different KMCs, they are different segments — however similar the parties look.**

R2 already states the rule at the level of the customer: *one focal cost per customer type.* The failure is that ventures partition the TAM FIRST — by who the claimant is, who the defendant is, what the dispute is about, which sector it sits in — and then attach a KMC to each segment afterwards. The KMC is fitted to a boundary that was drawn for a different reason. The result is a segment holding two, three or four different KMCs while claiming one, and nobody notices because the partition looks tidy.

**Why this is not a presentation problem.** Everything downstream inherits the boundary:
- the value ceiling is priced off one KMC and applied to sub-classes that do not have it
- the addressable market counts sub-classes whose KMC is already eliminated by a supplied alternative
- the product is designed for one entry point in the routine and sold to customers who need a different one
- the FIT gate passes, because FIT tests margin and never tests whether the segment holds one cost

**Run this before sizing anything:**

1. **Enumerate dispute sub-classes / use-case sub-classes**, not segments. The sub-class is the real unit. Go finer than feels necessary — the partition can always be merged upward, never split downward after the fact.
2. **Assign exactly one KMC to each sub-class.** If you cannot, the sub-class is still too coarse. Split it again.
3. **Run the supplied-alternative screen on each** (see Input 3). A sub-class whose KMC is eliminated by a supplied alternative gets **KMC-0** — no monetizable cost. It is unaddressable at any price and leaves the TAM.
4. **Group by KMC.** The groups ARE the segments. Name them by the cost, not by the parties: "enforcement gap", not "employment disputes".
5. **Check each resulting segment against the venture's qualifying gates** (the TAM-membership check above).

**The diagnostic that catches this in an existing record:** take any segment, list its sub-classes, and write the KMC beside each. If the column is not uniform, the partition is broken and every share quoted against it is unreliable.

*(Case: Calmly, 27 Aug 2026. Five segments partitioned by claimant/defendant/dispute-subject. On audit, S1 held three different KMCs, S2 held two, S3 two, S4 two, S5 three — every segment failed. The KMC of record — the valuation gap — did not hold in employment at all, where a free tribunal already supplies valuation and the real cost is non-collection. Four non-conformances, NCR-009 to NCR-012, all trace to this one defect.)*

### ⛔ PERSIST THE TAM AS A DATA OBJECT — prose is not a source of truth (added 27 Aug 2026, Tom's ruling)

**The TAM, its qualifying gates, its KMC classes, its sub-classes and their shares are written to a model file. Every downstream figure is generated from it or cited against it. No document re-states a market figure from memory.**

This is the same pattern the CTM and AOM already use: the YAML is the model, the views are generated, the generator refuses to write on an incoherent model. The TAM had no such object, so it lived as prose in a venture design record, was re-quoted into funder packs, one-pagers and valuation work, and drifted in each — with no way to check consistency short of reading every document.

**Required file:** `[venture]-tam-model.yaml`, alongside the CTM and AOM model files.

Not challenge-labelled (`at-C[N]`), unlike the CTM and AOM. The TAM is a PCO-level reference object — it changes when EVIDENCE changes, not when a challenge is re-run.

**Minimum contents:**

| Block | What it holds | Why it must be structured, not prose |
|---|---|---|
| `definition` | qualifying gates, each with an id and a rule | a gate applied at sizing but not carried through is invisible in prose |
| `volume` | the figure, its tier, its provenance, and **`basis: incidence` or `basis: residual`** | an undeclared basis is how a segment share gets divided by the wrong denominator |
| `kmc_classes` | every KMC, including **KMC-0 for "supplied alternative eliminates it"** | the partition key needs an identity before it can partition anything |
| `sub_classes` | one KMC each, one addressability verdict each, supplied alternative with sources, share with a tier | this is the real unit; segments are derived from it |
| `recorded_segments` | the legacy partition, retained with its declared `defect` | never silently delete a partition others still quote |
| `proposed_segments` | the KMC partition, one KMC per segment | the correction, and what it is blocked on |
| `surviving` | the arithmetic, reproducible from its own rows | a market size nobody can recompute is an assertion |
| `consumers` | every document quoting a TAM figure, each marked CURRENT / STALE / CHECK | a change here is untraceable downstream without it |

**Unmeasured is a legitimate value.** `share: {value: null, tier: unmeasured}` is a correct, deliberate state. It is not a gap to fill with a plausible number. The validator refuses a value carrying tier `unmeasured` — a number with no measurement behind it is a guess wearing a decimal point.

**Validate before any downstream use:**

```bash
python3 .claude/skills/shared/generators/validate_tam_model.py <path>/[venture]-tam-model.yaml
```

The checker enforces ten rules. The ones that do the most work: **L2** — a segment holds exactly one KMC (the partition rule above, made mechanical); **L6** — no invented shares; **L7** — the volume basis must be declared, and it warns when an incidence figure is paired with residual-defined sub-classes; **L9** — the surviving-TAM arithmetic must reproduce from its own rows. Fix the YAML. Never weaken the check.

## Input 1 — High-Import Outcome (HIO)

> *The job that holds the greatest importance to the greatest number of actors in the PCO that can be meaningfully impacted and credibly delivered by the venture.*

### What it is

The "job" that holds the greatest importance to the greatest number of actors in the PCO that can be meaningfully impacted and credibly delivered by the venture.

The HIO is the anchor for the entire R2 analysis. Every element of the Attendant Routines map, every cost identified, and the Efficacy Strategy all point back to the HIO.

### How to state an outcome correctly

**Frame in the first person — from the customer's perspective, as an "I" statement.** Not "the actor designs a business architecture" (outside-in, third-person) but "I design a business architecture" (inside-out, customer's voice). This is not cosmetic: first-person framing forces you to stay in the customer's experience and makes it harder to smuggle in a value proposition.

**A clinical first-person statement of what the customer is trying to achieve. No "how." No "why." No adverbs. No adjectives (one exception: "X is Y" when describing a state, not a process).**

| Wrong | Right | Why it matters |
|-------|-------|----------------|
| "I hire high-quality employees efficiently" | "I hire employees" or "I fill employee vacancies" | The wrong version imports a value proposition — you have already assumed your solution is the answer before diagnosing the problem |
| "I resolve my landlord dispute successfully" | "I recover my deposit" or "I reach a binding settlement" | "Successfully" is an adverb — it obscures what success means and makes cost identification ambiguous |
| "I get fast access to capital" | "I access capital" | "Fast" is your value proposition, not the outcome |
| "The actor designs a business architecture" | "I design a business architecture" | Third-person distances you from the customer's experience and makes it easier to drift into describing what the venture does rather than what the customer needs |

Stating the outcome with adverbs or adjectives — or in the third person — creates a circular argument that makes rigorous diagnosis impossible. Strip the outcome to its bare minimum, first-person statement first — then investigate the costs within it.

### Finding the right level — the bounding step (derive it, never assert it)

⛔ **A bare statement on the wrong rung is still wrong.** The rules above strip adjectives, adverbs and the value proposition out of the wording. They do **not** choose the level. Stripping the wording and stopping there is a known failure (logged 28 Aug 2026): the outcome came out correctly bare, sat one rung away from its own key monetizable cost, and nothing caught it because no ladder existed to check against.

**Why the level decides everything downstream.** The KMC is found inside the routine for the chosen outcome, so **the choice of outcome decides which costs exist at all**. Too low and you *truncate* — costs the venture genuinely removes fall outside the boundary and are never counted, understating your own ceiling. Too high and you *inflate* — the routine fills with costs the venture cannot touch, the value story stops being credible, and the deflators collapse. Every number after this point is computed against the boundary set here. Get it wrong and the cost floor, the ceiling and the FMOS are all measured against the wrong cost set — and they will look internally consistent while being wrong together.

**Authority.** The full procedure is Step 1a of the **Objective Customer Value standard** (`objective-customer-value-standard.md`, v1.2+, lint L14). Run it here in full; the OCV standard governs on any conflict. Five stages:

**Stage 1 — build the outcome ladder.** Start at the customer's apex outcome ("I run my business", "I run my home"). Decompose top-down into the bare outcomes it contains, **breadth-first — list every sibling at a level before descending into any of them.** Breadth-first is the anti-bias device: descend early and you drop straight into the branch the venture already occupies and never see the others. Two to four levels is normally enough. Every rung obeys the bare-statement rule above.

**Stage 2 — rank rungs by revealed importance.** Importance is never asked; it is read from what the customer already allocates. Per rung: what do they spend on its routine today, how much time does it consume, what do failures against it cost, what have they already bought to protect it? **A rung the customer says matters but allocates nothing to is not yet an outcome they will pay against.** Stated importance is cheap; revealed importance is evidence.

**Stage 3 — screen for deliverability.** Strike any rung whose routine the venture cannot materially change. Touching a trivial share of a routine's cost does not entitle you to claim that outcome, however important it is.

**Stage 4 — apply the minimal covering outcome rule.** From the rungs that survive:

> **Choose the lowest rung whose routine contains every cost the venture changes.**

This makes both failure directions mechanical. A cost the venture removes falling **outside** the chosen routine means the rung is too low — go up one. The chosen routine carrying large cost blocks the venture **cannot touch** means it is too high — check whether one rung down still covers everything, and drop if it does. **The correct level is usually one rung lower than first instinct, because solution-builders overstate their reach.**

⚠ **When no single rung covers, that is a scope finding, not a wording problem.** If the venture changes costs across several sibling rungs, no lower rung covers and the rule forces you up to the parent — which then carries cost blocks you cannot touch. Do not paper over this. It means the venture is claiming more outcomes than it has evidenced. Either narrow the claim to what the designed mechanism actually delivers, or accept the higher rung and its weaker deflators **explicitly**, and record which you chose.

**Stage 5 — validate and record.** The statement is bare; the customer would recognise it in their own words; success criteria are separated out; and **the covering test is written down — which costs, which rung, why.** An HIO with no ladder and no covering test on record is an L14 failure and does not pass this input.

**Required output — emit before the KMC is selected:**

```
OUTCOME LADDER — R2
apexOutcome: [the customer's top-level outcome]
ladder: [breadth-first tree, 2–4 levels, every rung bare]
struckRungs: [rung → why the venture cannot materially change it]
revealedImportance: [rung → what the customer already allocates to it]
costsVentureChanges: [each cost the designed mechanism removes or reduces]
chosenRung: [the outcome of record]
coveringTest: [every cost above sits inside this rung's routine — yes/no per cost]
kmcRungCheck: [the KMC sits on the chosen rung — true/false]  ← false = truncation; fix before proceeding
```

`kmcRungCheck: false` is a hard stop. The outcome and its key monetizable cost must sit on the same rung.

## Input 2 — Attendant Routines

> *The routines within which the key monetizable cost is embedded — mapped across DO, USE/BUY, THINK, and FEEL.*

### What they are

The routines within which the key monetizable cost is embedded. Mapping them is the *mechanism* for discovering that cost — it cannot be found by asking customers directly.

### The four components

For each step in the customer's routine, capture all four:

| Component | What to observe | Why it matters |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| **DO** | The sequence of activities and actions. What they physically do, in order, to try to achieve the outcome. Observed, not reported. | The cost is usually hidden in the sequencing and in the activities customers do not notice they are doing |
| **USE / BUY** | The tools, products, services, and inputs deployed. What they spend money on. What infrastructure they rely on. | Reveals money costs and dependency structure — what they cannot substitute |
| **THINK** | The concepts, beliefs, and mental models applied. How they make decisions. What heuristics they use. What they worry about. | Reveals fear costs — the weight of uncertainty, risk of failure, and the consequences of not achieving the job |
| **FEEL** | The emotions accompanying the activities. Anxiety before a step. Relief when it succeeds. Resignation when it fails. | Diagnostic signals for stress costs — and for the moments where the cost is experienced most acutely |

### Per-dimension probe questions

Use these in fieldwork and in reasoning through the Attendant Routines map. For each routine step:

| Dimension | Probe questions |
|-----------|----------------|
| **KPI / outcome** | What does success look like for this step? How do you know when it goes well? What goes wrong most often — and how often? |
| **DO** | Walk me through exactly what you do. What happens first? Then what? Who else is involved? What do you do when it fails? |
| **USE / BUY** | What tools, products, or services do you rely on here? What do you spend money on? What would break if you removed it? |
| **THINK** | What decisions are you making at this step? What do you worry about? What would going wrong here cost you? |
| **FEEL** | How do you feel when you are doing this? When it goes wrong, what is that like? What is the relief when it works? |

### The Autopilot Problem

Routines are "routine" — customers are in autopilot and unaware of most of what they do. **The customer cannot tell you their routines directly.** You have to observe them performing the activity and reconstruct the routine from what you see, then test your reconstruction with the customer.

What customers report when asked about their routines is usually the visible, conscious, non-habitual parts. The key cost lives in the habitual, invisible, unexamined parts — the steps so ingrained that customers have adapted around them without realising there was another way.

**The gap between what customers say they do and what they actually do is where the key cost lives.**

## Input 3 — Key Monetizable Cost

> *The single greatest monetizable cost — in money, fear, or stress — that the solution eliminates for the target customer.*

### What it is

The single greatest monetizable cost — in money, fear, or stress — that the solution eliminates for the target customer. There should be a single key cost, not a basket. At the architectural level, one focal cost per customer type. Targeting multiple costs increases uncertainty, creates fuzzy KPIs, and diffuses the value proposition before the market exists.

**Single source, not a basket of problems.** Ventures that attempt to solve multiple costs for the same customer create ambiguity about what the product is for, make it impossible to build a clean Efficacy Theory of Change, and produce KPIs that cannot be read clearly. Platform businesses are not an exception: they have one key cost per side (e.g. Airbnb: hosts have one cost — sunk-asset foregone income; guests have one cost — accommodation price risk), not a combined basket. Identify one focal cost per customer type and design the Efficacy Strategy to eliminate that cost completely before considering secondary costs.

### Three cost types

**Money Cost**
Cash spent or foregone to achieve the outcome. Includes direct expenditure on inputs, indirect costs (opportunity cost of time), and revenue lost due to current routine limitations. Example: revenues 50% lower because farmers lack upfront cash for fertilizer they cannot afford.

Money cost has two sub-types — and the **foregone sub-type is frequently larger and should be identified separately before deciding which to target:**

| Sub-type | Definition | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expended** | Cash the customer is currently paying to manage the problem | Consultant fees of £50–150k to get a business architecture | Already budgeted; directly displaced by the venture; easiest to price against |
| **Foregone** | Value the customer is *not* capturing because the problem exists — revenue, exit value, income, or opportunity blocked by the current state | Farmer's revenues 50% lower than possible; Airbnb host earning £0 from a sunk-cost asset; founder-capped owner receiving a 2–3× exit multiple vs the 5–8× they'd get with a scalable architecture | Often *larger* in magnitude than expended; requires the customer to see the causal link clearly |

**The Airbnb precedent (Simanis, *Built to Hold*).** The key value bottleneck Airbnb solved was entirely a foregone cost — hosts had a sunk-cost asset generating zero incremental income. There was no "expended" money to displace. WTP ($350–$755/stay) was set against the foregone income, not an existing expenditure. This is the canonical case that foregone money is a legitimate and often primary price-ceiling driver.

**Causal-link requirement for foregone cost.** Foregone cost only converts to WTP if the customer can see that *this specific bottleneck* is what's blocking the value. If they can't draw the causal line — "because I lack X, I'm missing Y" — the foregone cost stays abstract and won't anchor a price. The Efficacy Strategy (SR6) must make that link visible and credible.

**When both sub-types are present, aim to eliminate both.** Do not artificially pick one. The expended cost anchors near-term WTP (conservative ceiling); the foregone cost anchors full WTP (ambitious ceiling). Designing the Efficacy Strategy to eliminate both raises the ceiling and creates a stronger value proposition. State which sub-type sets the price ceiling you're designing to, and which remains as upside.

⛔ **Misclassification guard — "venture-created value" (ruled by Tom, 27 Aug 2026).** A foregone loss counts as KMC even when the customer cannot escape it alone — needing the venture's mechanism to capture the value does NOT reclassify the loss as "venture-created value outside WTP." The customer bears the loss today; that is the whole test. The error pattern: an analyst sees that the customer has no route to the value without the venture and concludes the value is new rather than foregone — and thereby caps WTP at the expended component. Do not do this. (Case: Calmly site WTP, 27 Aug 2026 — foregone trust-revenue was wrongly classed as venture-created and excluded from Simanis WTP; corrected same day. Opportunity-cost losses are often the largest component — the Airbnb precedent above is the canonical proof.)

⛔ **Check for a free or better route before you accept any KMC (added 27 Aug 2026).** A cost only counts if the customer still carries it. If the customer already has a free or better way to deal with the problem — a government scheme, a free complaints service, a cheap tribunal, or a lawyer who takes the case and pays them more than you would — then you cannot charge to solve it, at any price. Those customers must come out of the market size. Check each type of problem separately: a free route usually covers one part of a market and not the rest.

**Do not confuse two different things:**
- **Coping is not solving.** A customer who insures the loss, absorbs it, or gives up still carries the cost. These customers stay in the market, and what they do instead is usually the biggest cost in the routine (see the guard above).
- **A free or better route does solve it.** The test: does the other route remove the problem itself? If a scheme already holds the disputed money and decides the case for free, there is nothing left to sell, however good the product is.

(Case: Calmly S1 housing, 27 Aug 2026 — tenancy deposits are held by a scheme that decides disputes free; deposit-penalty claims have a no-win-no-fee market paying the claimant ~75% against our ~25% offer. The segment passed every FIT gate and kept ~25–30% of the market while two of its four problem types had nothing left to sell. FIT tests margin. It does not test whether anyone needs you.)

**Sizing method — routine comparison (corrected by Tom, 27 Aug 2026).** Size value by comparing ROUTINES, never components in isolation: take the customer's pre-intervention routine and the routine they would run with the C2 Business Form Factor in place, then sum every cost the new routine eliminates AND every new revenue or value stream it generates. That full delta is the value construction (OCV = EV(with) − EV(current)). Within it, **revealed market prices are the strongest evidence for valuing a delta component** — when any live market already charges customers to remove the same loss, that observed price outranks estimates bridged from academic studies (a welfare figure is not a revenue figure). The comparison of routines is the method; the revealed price is an input to it, not a substitute for it. (Case: a £0.10–0.40 band bridged from a welfare study vs a live market clearing at ~£1.60 for the same loss class — both belong inside the routine delta, valued at the revealed price.)

⛔ **Foregone loss IS Simanis WTP — the taxonomy settles it (ruled by Tom, 27 Aug 2026).** Do not construct a dichotomy between "Simanis WTP" and "opportunity-cost value." Simanis anchors WTP to the loss the customer escapes in its current routine, and **foregone is one of the four cost classes in that routine** — the taxonomy is `Expended · Foregone · Risk-borne · Friction` (OCV standard §2.2, Simanis doctrine applied; `EV = Σ[p×outcome] − C_expended − C_foregone − C_risk − C_friction`). A foregone loss is therefore inside WTP by construction, not adjacent to it. **A loss does not stop being a loss because the customer lacks the means to escape it alone — that is precisely the kind of loss ventures monetize.**

⛔ **Same class, larger measurement — never a new category.** When a bigger number appears for a loss you have already sized, **it re-sizes the existing component; it does not create a second one.** The error pattern: an analyst finds a revealed price well above the recorded band, cannot reconcile it with the recorded figure, and invents a separate category ("upside beyond WTP", "venture-created value") to hold it — which quietly removes it from the ceiling. Both numbers describe one thing. State it that way: *"the record sized [component] at [band] from [weaker evidence]; [revealed source] re-sizes the same component at [larger figure]."*

⛔ **Size a foregone loss as blocked RETURN × capture probability — never as the spend that did not happen.** The guard above stops foregone value being excluded. This rule stops it being *mis-sized*, which produces the same exclusion by a different route. Two symmetrical errors:
  - *Excluding it* — "the business chose not to spend that money, so no venture can charge against a purchase that did not happen." Wrong: the loss is not the un-made purchase.
  - *Entering it at face value* — booking the whole un-made investment as the loss. Also wrong, and it inflates the ceiling with a number that cannot convert.

  **Both come from measuring the wrong quantity.** The foregone loss is the **value the blocked activity would have produced**, not the outlay that was avoided. Per the OCV standard §2.2: *size the blocked value × the probability the solution actually unblocks it.* An un-made £6,000 investment is not a £6,000 loss and it is not a £0 loss — it is `£6,000 × return rate × P(the venture actually unblocks it)`. Record the return rate and the capture probability as separate, flagged parameters. Entering a foregone item with no capture probability is an OCV lint failure (L5). **A foregone loss that cannot be sized this way is recorded as an assumption with its two parameters named — never dropped, and never entered gross.**

**How to carry the two figures — gate on the evidenced number, state the permitted ceiling.** Do not average them, and do not silently adopt the larger one. Write both, with the condition that would promote the larger:
> *WTP as recorded is [conservative figure]. The method permits the ceiling to rise to [larger figure] once the foregone component is evidenced at the revealed scale. The gates use [conservative figure] until then.*

The promotion condition must name the specific test that would convert it — an A/B at a live site, a first-portfolio measurement, an actual quoted price. A permitted ceiling with no named conversion test is an aspiration, not a ceiling.

⛔ **Blended KMC — decompose by cost class and gate on evidenced conversion (ruled by Tom, 1 Sep 2026 — VA-74).** A measured KMC often blends cost classes: cash paid to third parties, the customer's own time, fear premia. Before the gate figure is set, **decompose the KMC into its class components and gate on the components whose conversion to WTP is evidenced.** The criterion is evidence, not class:
  - **Cash paid to third parties converts by default** — it is an existing outflow the venture displaces.
  - **A time or fear component enters the gate basis only with named conversion evidence** — a revealed price (e.g. the customer already pays a third party to be rid of the burden), a billed-hours loss (time that is invoiceable revenue, which is foregone money cost by the taxonomy, not stress), or a measured trade-off. Otherwise it sits on the permitted basis with its promotion test.
  - **Two symmetric failures, both banned:** gating on a blended figure without decomposing it (silently counts unevidenced time as if it were cash — the v1 SME Trade Record error); and gating on cash *as a class rule* (silently excludes time value that genuinely converts — time is not uniformly stress-class).
  A component is never silently inside the gate figure and never silently dropped: every component appears in the decomposition with its class, its conversion evidence or its promotion test.

**Fear Cost**
The worry of failing to achieve the outcome given current routines. The psychological weight of uncertainty, risk of failure, and the consequences of not achieving the job. Monetizable through the premium customers pay to reduce risk — but only when the fear maps to a specific, measurable failure mode with real consequences.

**Stress Cost**
The difficulty of managing and executing current routines. Cognitive load, coordination effort, time drain. Monetizable primarily when it results in worse outcomes, not just discomfort. Pure stress reduction without outcome improvement is the hardest to monetize in a new market.

### Monetizability spectrum

The three cost types are not equally monetizable. Ranking (easiest to hardest to convert to WTP):

| Cost type | Characteristic | Monetizability | Risk |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|------|
| **Money cost** | Objective + outcome-fungible — the cash freed up can be redirected anywhere | Highest | Low — the customer controls the saving and can spend it immediately |
| **Fear cost** | Subjective + outcome-enabling — the anxiety reduction enables a better result | Medium | Medium — requires demonstrated causal link between fear reduction and outcome |
| **Stress cost** | Subjective + outcome-neutral — the relief does not improve performance on the outcome | Lowest | High — stress reduction without outcome improvement almost never creates a new market |

**Why money is easiest to monetize:** the saving is objective (not dependent on the customer's interpretation) and fungible (they can redirect the cash to anything). The customer can price the loss precisely and confirm that it was eliminated.

**Why stress is hardest:** stress reduction that does not change outcomes is invisible to the rational evaluation that drives WTP in a new market. It may be a strong competitive differentiator once the market exists. It is not a market-creation driver.

**The adoption threshold.** The size of the cost determines the speed of adoption. If the cost you have identified is small, adoption will be slow regardless of solution quality. The threshold question to ask before committing to a KMC: "Is there enough suffering here that eliminating it would compel a routine change?" If the answer requires qualifications, the cost is probably too small.

**The customer cannot tell you their key cost directly.** They have already normalised it — they have built the best routine they can and have no view of what else is possible. What they report when asked is likely a small, visible cost, not the key structural one. The key cost is usually invisible to the customer because they have adapted around it.

### Sizing the Key Monetizable Cost

Benchmark against comparables. Calculate the total value assuming the solution is delivered for free — this sets the ceiling of the value curve. The gap between this ceiling and the R1-determined cost floor is the theoretical maximum value surplus.

Express as: `Key Monetizable Cost ≈ [Magnitude per customer] × [PCO population affected]`

This produces the aggregate addressable value — the size of the market opportunity, not just the value per unit.

## Input 4 — Efficacy Theory of Change + Efficacy Strategy

> *The parameter on which reduction of the key cost rests, and the best way to operationalize it.*

### Efficacy Theory of Change

The parameter on which reduction or elimination of the key cost rests, and the theory most effective in explaining how to solve for that parameter.

**Format (same four fields as R1 ToC):**
- **Class of problem:** what structural mechanism produces the key cost? (information asymmetry, coordination failure, adverse incentives, behavioural lock-in, market structure)
- **Theory:** cite the peer-reviewed theory that predicts how addressing this mechanism changes behaviour
- **Current state:** how the key cost currently manifests — specific, observable
- **Desired state:** the state after the key cost is eliminated — as a completed state, not an activity

**Discipline:** theory section contains only established, citable findings. The design claim — why *this* approach eliminates *this* cost — belongs in the Efficacy Strategy.

**Use `/theory-of-change-custom` for the full guided process.** When scoping, tell it: "R2 Efficacy." It will produce a named **Efficacy Theory of Change** using the CMO structure (Pawson & Tilley, 1997), enforce the theory/hypothesis distinction at each step, and output the four fields in BALM-ready format.

### Efficacy Strategy

**The Efficacy Strategy sets out what is REQUIRED to eliminate the Key Monetizable Cost** (ruled by Tom, 28 Aug 2026). It is a specification of conditions, not a direction of travel and not a description of a product.

⛔ **Eliminate, not reduce.** The same standard R1 applies to the CLO applies here. "Meaningful reduction" is not the bar — a cost reduced is a cost the customer still carries, and the ceiling is set by what is actually removed. If the strategy only reduces the KMC, say so plainly and state the residual the customer keeps paying; do not describe a reduction as an elimination.

**The strategy answers one question: what must be true for this cost to stop existing?** Each answer is a *condition* — something the architecture must make true, that is not true today. Conditions are testable and can each be false. "We will make it easy" is not a condition. "The party who owes the money confirms the amount at the moment of the trade" is.

**Derive the conditions from the cost, not from the product.** Take the KMC and ask what the customer is actually paying for. Each element of that cost gives a condition. Work from the cost outward. If a condition cannot be traced back to a specific element of the KMC, it is a product feature that has arrived early — strike it, or find the cost element it serves.

⛔ **REQUIRED OUTPUT — emit with the Efficacy Strategy. Strategy prose alone is not valid.**

```
ELIMINATION CONDITIONS — R2
kmc: [the cost being eliminated, with its measured or assumed value]
conditions:
  C1: [what must be true] | costElementRemoved: [which part of the KMC this removes]
      | trueToday: false | whoMakesItTrue: [venture / customer / counterparty / partner]
      | testable: [what observation would show this condition holds]
  C2: ...
residual: [what part of the KMC survives even when every condition holds — state the £ or state none]
eliminationClaim: [full / partial] ← partial requires the residual above to be quantified
```

**Two checks on the block before it passes:**

1. **Every condition names who makes it true.** A condition nobody owns is a wish. If the answer is "the counterparty" or "a partner", that is not a defect — but it is now visible, and it becomes a requirement on R7 (gateway) rather than an assumption buried in R2.
2. **The conditions together must cover the KMC.** Sum the cost elements the conditions remove. If they do not account for the whole KMC, the elimination claim is partial and the residual must be stated in money. An unstated residual is how a partial elimination gets priced as a full one, which inflates the ceiling.

The best way to operationalise the Efficacy ToC to eliminate the key cost.

⚠ **MANDATORY — before writing the Efficacy Strategy, complete these three steps in order:**

**Step 4a — Baseline honestly.** State where the current BFF stands on eliminating the Key Monetizable Cost. Not "not yet addressed" and not "already solved" — what does it actually do, and what does it leave unresolved?

**Step 4c — Evaluate and decide.** For each candidate: what does it gain on eliminating the KMC? What does it cost in coherence with R1? Record the decision. Adopt a mutation (restate the evolved BFF) or hold the current one with explicit reasons each alternative was rejected. Never "it already works."

**Critical constraint:** the Efficacy Strategy must be consistent with the Workaround Strategy from R1. You are designing the value curve around an architecture that already has an R1 solution — the Efficacy Strategy must work within or build on the BFF the Workaround Strategy established.

**The architectural decision test:** a Strategy answer names the single structural decision that makes the Theory of Change fire in this specific venture context — not a description of what will happen as a result. Operational consequences (what will happen if the strategy works) are evidence the strategy is correct; they are not the strategy itself. Ask: "Is this an architectural decision, or a description of outcomes?" If the answer lists what will happen rather than naming the structural move that causes it, the strategy has not been found.

### Worked example — farmer input finance

| Element | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Key Monetizable Cost** | Money cost — **foregone sub-type**: revenues 50% lower because farmers lack upfront cash for fertilizer and seeds. Can grow twice as much with inputs they currently cannot afford. ## Productizing R2 — Strategy → BFF

The Efficacy Strategy names the mechanism that eliminates the Key Monetizable Cost. Productizing it means shaping the Working Product so the mechanism fires automatically when the customer uses it — not through a skilled operator or a separate intervention layer.

**Target product component:** Working Product (Using — value realisation)

Work through these three questions before writing the cumulative BFF:

**1. Architecture, not operations.** Is the efficacy mechanism delivered by the product form factor, or by a trained human? If a skilled facilitator, advisor, or coach is required to produce the efficacy outcome, the product has not been productized — it has been staffed. Ask: what product design would produce the same outcome without the operator? Staffing costs scale linearly. Architectural efficacy does not.

**2. Accumulation.** Does the Efficacy Strategy work within the BFF shape R1 established — or does it require changing the Workaround form factor? Either is valid, but if R2's productizing requires a change to R1's form, update the R1 BFF first and then write R2. Confirm the R1 consistency check from the Efficacy Strategy step is satisfied before proceeding.

**3. Value ceiling alignment.** Does the productized form factor actually eliminate the Key Monetizable Cost at the scale R2 was designed for? A mechanism that works in a supported pilot but requires operational scaffolding at scale has not been productized. The BFF must be able to deliver the efficacy outcome self-sufficiently.

**4. At-scale test.** Is this mechanism designed for the venture operating at capital-payback scale — the volume at which all required investment is paid back at the required IRR — or is it designed for the first cohort? A BFF that requires founding-team bandwidth, managed-by-exception operations, or pilot-only concessions is a launch-phase design, not an architecture. Name the at-scale volume explicitly. If the mechanism breaks before reaching it, return to Diagnose.

When all three are confirmed, identify the architectural spine before writing anything.

**Step 0 — Find the architectural spine.** The architectural spine is the single mechanism that runs through all prior requirements — the element whose removal would cascade failures across the most of C1 through C[N]. Every BFF has one. Finding it before writing forces the statement to be an architecture, not a list.

Diagnostic question: what would break first if you removed one core element from the design? The mechanism that cascades failures across the most requirements is the spine.

Write the opening sentence of the BFF around it. If the opening sentence could be "A product that does X (C1); does Y (C2); does Z (C3)..." — the spine has not been found. Go back and find it.

Write the cumulative BFF (C1–C2). Then confirm it with the user before proceeding.

## Research methods for R2

| Data type needed | Best method | Notes |
|-----------------|-------------|-------|
| Basic facts — demographics, usage frequencies | Survey / questionnaire | Fast and scalable but limited to what customers can report consciously. Use for cluster sizing only. |
| Simple activities — step sequences, decision points | Phone or video interview | Good for reconstructing routine structure. Probe for specifics, not generalities. |
| Feelings — fears, stress points, emotional triggers | In-depth 1:1 interview | Requires trust and rapport. Follow-up questions are critical: "tell me more about that." |
| Complex routines — multi-step, multi-actor, hard to articulate | Ethnographic observation | Observe in context. The gap between what customers say they do and what they actually do is where the key cost lives. |

### Question types to avoid

**Leading questions:** "Do you find that...?" / "Is X important to you?" / "Would you like...?" — these import the answer into the question.

**Yes/no questions:** "Is it hard to...?" / "Would you buy...?" — these require no reasoning and reveal nothing about cost structure.

**Guessable-intent questions:** any question that can be answered by inferring what the researcher wants to hear. Customers are socially intelligent — they will tell you what they think you want to know, not what they actually experience.

## Common failure patterns at R2

| Failure | What it looks like | What it means |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|
| Benefits framing | "Our product is faster and easier" | Not eliminating a cost — improving an experience. Benefits (faster / easier / better) confuse a USP with a cost. They have very low monetizability in a new market because they don't reduce the customer's loss — they improve what already works. |
| Multiple key costs | "We solve five different pain points" | One focal cost per customer type. Multiple targets create fuzzy KPIs and diffuse the value proposition. |
| Customer-reported cost | "They told us the biggest problem is X" | The customer has normalised the structural cost. What they report is the visible cost, not the key one. Requires routine observation. |
| Outcome stated with adverbs | "Recover their deposit quickly" | "Quickly" is the value proposition smuggled into the outcome. Strip it — then diagnose the cost of the current routine. |
| R2 designed without R1 | Customer value proposition built around what customers want | Produces a value proposition incompatible with the cost structure the R1 architecture creates. |
| Stress cost as anchor | "We reduce the stress of the process" | Stress reduction without outcome improvement is the hardest to monetize in a new market. Only works once the market is established. |
| TAM partitioned by parties, not by cost | Segments named "housing disputes", "employment disputes", "marketplace disputes" — by who sues whom. A KMC is attached to each afterwards. | The boundary was drawn for a reason unrelated to cost, so segments hold several KMCs while claiming one. Partition by KMC first; name segments by the cost. See the TAM partition rule above. |
| TAM held as prose | The market figure lives in a venture design record and is re-quoted into every downstream document | Each re-quote drifts, and consistency cannot be checked without reading everything. Persist the TAM as a model file and cite it. |
| Operational description as Strategy | Listing outcomes ("multiple providers compete", "fees fall", "customers persist") instead of naming the architectural decision that produces them. An outcome is evidence the strategy works — it is not the strategy. | Name the single structural move whose removal would make the outcome impossible. That is the architectural decision. Everything else is consequence. |
| Semicolon chain as BFF statement | "A product that does X (C1); does Y (C2); does Z (C3)..." | A semicoloned list of challenge solutions is a checklist, not a BFF. The statement should describe one coherent product architecture. Find the architectural spine — the single mechanism whose removal would cascade failures across the most prior requirements — and write the opening sentence around it. The challenge labels should appear as parenthetical citations, not as the structural joints holding the sentence together. |

## Generative discipline — mutate the BFF, don't justify it (VA-1 / VA-3 / VA-4)

Reference: `method/context/va-design-discipline.md`

This challenge's job is **not** to confirm the current BFF already handles customer value / efficacy — the value ceiling. It is to find out whether changing the BFF could handle it **materially better**. Treat the current BFF as the baseline to beat — not the answer to defend.

**VA-3 — generate before you justify.** Before writing this challenge's Strategy:
1. **Baseline, honestly.** Where does the current BFF stand on customer value / efficacy — the value ceiling? Not zero, not solved.
2. **Generative target.** "How could we change the BFF to do materially better on customer value / efficacy — the value ceiling than the baseline?"
3. **Generate ≥2–3 candidate BFF mutations** — structural changes to the business, not features bolted on.
   ⚠ If you cannot produce two genuine mutations, say so explicitly. That is the tell you have slipped into justification mode — do not paper over it by restating the existing BFF.
4. **Evaluate each against the whole** — gain on customer value / efficacy — the value ceiling vs cost to coherence (VA-1) and to dimensions earned in earlier challenges.
5. **Decide and record** — adopt a mutation (restate the evolved BFF) or hold the current one with explicit reasons each alternative was rejected. Never "it already works."

**VA-4 — show your working.** The candidates considered and rejected are not scratch work. Record them in the "considered / not chosen" block of the output format so the choice is auditable. Where a sub-requirement selects among options, first consider 2–3 — across the relevant customers/partners — then record which won and why the others lost.

**VA-1 — synthesis test.** When the (possibly evolved) BFF is stated, anchor it: "this now looks like [real business], because [shared mechanism]." If no clean analogue exists, flag it — the parts have not yet cohered into one business.

## Process discipline

These rules apply to every session working through this skill's sub-requirements.

### 1 — One field at a time, strictly gated

For each sub-requirement, the sequence is fixed:
1. **Display the definition** — output the sub-requirement number, name, and its full definition verbatim as a blockquote, exactly as written in this skill document
2. **Then ask** — only after the definition is shown, invite the user to respond
3. **Never reverse this order** — do not propose a solution, suggest an answer, or ask a question until the definition has been displayed

Do not move to the next sub-requirement until the current one is confirmed by the user. If the user drifts into the next sub-requirement, name the drift and complete the current one first.

### 2 — No design solutions until the Theory of Change is complete

The Strategy cell is locked until class of problem, theory, context, and outcome are all confirmed. Do not propose mechanisms, tactics, or architectural moves during Diagnose or Theorize. Design thinking before theory produces solutions without foundations.

### 3 — Theory stress-testing before acceptance

Before accepting any theory as the basis for a ToC, ask: has this been undermined by the replication crisis? Is there a Peters/ergodicity critique that reframes the finding as rational rather than biased? Peer-reviewed theories that have been substantially challenged should not be used as primary theoretical anchors. If a theory is contested, name the challenge and find a more robust alternative before proceeding.

### 4 — Cross-requirement elegance: check the sequencing

When claiming that a previous requirement's mechanism also solves the current one, verify that the mechanism actually reaches the customer at the right moment in their use routine. A mechanism that fires at the wrong time — after the decision point, or before the customer encounters the block — does not solve the current requirement, even if it addresses the same underlying problem.

### 5 — Distinguish three questions that challenge fields often conflate

When diagnosing any block or bottleneck, keep these three questions separate:
- **Where does it manifest?** — the specific step in the use routine or operating model where the block appears
- **What is the main factor causing it?** — the underlying factor, not the location
- **What class of problem does it represent?** — the theoretical category that determines which body of research applies

These belong in different fields. Collapsing them produces vague diagnoses and mismatched theories.

### 6 — No forward references (VA-80)

Solve this challenge with what exists at this challenge: the PCO and the solutions of the challenges before it. Do not use a later challenge's mechanism, actor or vocabulary to close a gap here. If a condition cannot be met with what exists, record it as a residual carried to the later requirement by number, and say nothing about how that requirement solves it. A cell that names an actor or mechanism the architecture has not yet introduced is not complete; it is borrowing.

### 7 — Credibility mechanisms: name the receiver, the instrument and the supplier (VA-81)

Whenever a cell relies on an element whose job is to make an actor believe something — an endorsement, a certification, a signal, a warranty, a published record, a guarantee, "independent" anything — the cell is not complete until it states, in this order:

1. **Receiver.** The named actor whose belief changes, and the decision that belief changes (settle sooner; buy the portfolio; integrate the product).
2. **Proposition.** The one thing the receiver must accept as true.
3. **Instrument in use today.** What that receiver relies on now to accept propositions of this kind, with evidence. Their routine of trust, not ours.
4. **Instrument test.** The element must be that instrument, or a substitute the receiver already accepts. A signal the receiver cannot read is not a signal: it must be costly to fake and cheap for *that receiver* to read (Spence).
5. **Supplier test.** At least one named organisation sells or issues that instrument today, shown from public record *before* the element is admitted. If none does, the element is a component the venture must build and cost, not a partner to be found; write it as such.
6. **Re-derivation.** If a later challenge changes the receiver or the decision, the element is re-run at that challenge. It is never inherited by its label.

Properties of the provider — "independent", "no stake", "standing" — are necessary and never sufficient; "standing" has no meaning until the receiver is named. An element a later challenge depends on may not be deferred to "implementation".

*Failure this cures (Calmly C3, Aug 2026):* the cell required "an independent body with sufficient standing"; the shortlist chose CEDR by fit and speed; nobody asked which actor had to be convinced, what that actor reads, or whether CEDR sells the thing. C4 then moved the receiver from the defendant to the funder and the element was carried by label through C4–C10.

### Section 8 — FIT Verifier Gate *(gate: fin-sim updated)*

Run the financial margin of safety calculation across all four cost layers. R2 is the first definitive FMOS check — the KMC from this requirement is the price ceiling. Display it in this format:

```
⛔ **THE CARRYING CHECK — the KMC must carry the whole venture, not just the operating floor (ruled by Tom, 28 Aug 2026).**

R2 sets the price ceiling. The question that decides the venture is not "does the KMC exceed today's operating cost?" — it is **"is the eliminated cost large enough to support a price that carries the entire venture cost structure, including the capital?"** Those are different questions with different answers, and R2 was only asking the first.

**Two required prices. They are not the same number and the larger one governs.**

| Bar | Formula | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating bar** | cost floor × 1.25 | Can the venture run at a 25% margin of safety over its C1–C2 operating cost? |
| **Capital bar** | from the PCO investment parameters: `(capital × (1+requiredIRR)^years × terminalDiscount ÷ netMargin) ÷ (groupSize × penetration)` | Can the venture repay all investment capital at the required return? |

**The binding required price is the higher of the two. Compute both. Report both. Gate on the higher.**

⚠ **Why this is not a refinement.** The operating bar is computed on a floor that **excludes scaling capital by construction** (SC requires R3 — see the breakdown below). It is therefore an incomplete floor, and a required price derived from it is an underestimate of what the venture must actually charge. A run that gates only on the operating bar can report a very large FMOS while having almost no headroom on the number that binds. **Logged case, 27 Aug 2026:** operating bar £145, capital bar £714, ceiling £999 — the run reported FMOS 761% and read as overwhelming strength, while the true headroom was £285 and the design failed outright at the bottom of its own plausible margin band. The FMOS was not wrong; it was answering the smaller question.

⛔ **REQUIRED OUTPUT — emit with the FIT gate.**

```
CARRYING CHECK — R2
kmc:                     £[the derived ceiling]
operatingBar:            £[cost floor × 1.25]
capitalBar:              £[from PCO parameters — restate the parameters used]
bindingBar:              £[max of the two] | which: [operating / capital]
headroomOnBinding:       £[kmc − bindingBar]
carries:                 true/false        ← false = the KMC cannot support the venture
pcoEstimateVsDerived:    [PCO estimated the loss at £X; derived KMC is £Y; delta Z%]
floorCompleteness:       [SC excluded — this bar is a LOWER BOUND until R3 sizes it]
```

**`carries: false` is a hard stop and it is an R2 failure, not an R3 problem.** The eliminated cost is too small to support the venture at the required return. Do not proceed to R3 hoping the numbers improve — R3 adds cost, it does not add ceiling. Return to Diagnose: either the KMC is on the wrong rung and a larger cost sits one level up, or the wrong customer type was selected, or the investment quantum is wrong for this opportunity.

**Also re-run the PCO screen with the derived number.** The PCO screened use cases on a loss-to-price ratio using an *estimated* loss. R2 replaces that estimate with a derived KMC. **Re-run the screen.** If the derived KMC moves the ratio materially — in either direction — say so and state whether the PCO's selection still holds. A PCO selected on an estimate that the derived figure contradicts is a selection that needs revisiting, not a footnote. ```
```
