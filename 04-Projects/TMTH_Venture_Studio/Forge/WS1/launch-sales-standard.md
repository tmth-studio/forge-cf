## 1 · The frame — Part 1 of the book, done by the venture's own operator

Kazanjy's book has two parts. Part 1 is "doing the basics correctly, yourself. Going from 0 to ~30 customers." Part 2 is getting other people to do it. **Every venture in this studio is in Part 1 until it has 30 paying customers.** Nobody is hired to sell, no agency is engaged and no tool is bought before that line is crossed. This is Kazanjy's rule and it also follows from the no-cash rule (Tom, 2 September 2026).

One adaptation, and it is structural. In the book the founder sends the emails. In this studio the founder does not (Tom, 2 September 2026: *"really, really don't have to do cold sales outreach"*). The venture's operating identity — Finrad, from the venture's own mailbox, under its one-line disclosure — sends every first contact and every follow-up. Tom appears at the first reply that needs a person, at terms outside the envelope, at signature and at spend. So "founding sales" here means: **the founder's credibility, executed by the venture's operator.** Section 5 says how the founder's credibility is used without spending the founder's time or social capital.

## 2 · The sales narrative comes first, and it is derived, not written

Kazanjy: nothing goes out until the venture can answer seven questions "as crisply as possible." Every one of them is already answered in the venture design record. So the narrative is derived from the record, per `derivation-standard.md`. Writing it fresh is how sales copy drifts from the design.

| Kazanjy's question | Where the answer already lives |
|---|---|
| 1. What is the problem? | R2 — the high-import outcome and the key monetizable cost |
| 2. Who has the problem? | PCO — the customer with the specific functional responsibility; R7 — the gateway partner if the customer is reached through one |
| 3. What are the costs of not solving it? | R2 — the KMC, priced; the baseline-cost note (what they pay today) |
| 4. How do people solve it now? | R1 — the conventional business form factor; R4 — the alternatives table |
| 5. What has changed? | R1 — the workaround of the critical limiting operation; the "what has changed" line in the PCO |
| 6. How does the new solution work? | The BFF, stated in customer terms; the synthesis analogue ("like X but for Y, because Z") |
| 7. How do you know it is better? | The six levels of proof in section 5 — chosen by the venture's state on the send date |

**Test before use** (Kazanjy): say the problem statement, question 1, to one person in the industry. If they do not recognise it in their own words within a sentence, the narrative is not ready and no email is.

## 3 · Prospecting — the list is a derivation too

Kazanjy's list-building rule: pick the people with functional responsibility for the problem, then find **demand indicators** — observable facts that say this account has the problem now (open roles, traffic, a public complaint, a regulatory date). In this studio:

- The **target population** is the PCO's customer definition, partitioned as the TAM was partitioned (by key monetizable cost, never by convenience — see the TAM rule in the feedback log).
- The **demand indicator** for each account is recorded in the pipeline record (section 7) before the first email. An account with no indicator is not a prospect yet; it is a name.
- **List size for launch:** enough to reach 30 customers at the response and close rates the fin-sim assumes. If the fin-sim has no such rates, use Kazanjy's figures as the assumed input, flagged: 12 per cent reply to a personalised first email, 5 per cent to an unpersonalised one.

## 4 · The first email — structure and rules

Kazanjy gives no rigid template. He gives an order and a set of rules. Both are adopted.

**The order, five parts, in one screen:**

1. **The research line** — one sentence that proves the account was looked at: the demand indicator, in their words where possible. Not "I came across your company."
2. **The problem** — question 1 of the narrative, one or two sentences, in the prospect's vocabulary. The passive-step value from `sales-marketing-asset-standard.md` Rule 1 belongs here.
3. **What we do** — question 6, outcome first, no feature list. Two sentences at most.
4. **The proof line** — one line, from the six levels in section 5. One only.
5. **The ask** — see below.

**The ask — two asks, both in the first message.** Kazanjy: at this stage "you're simply selling a conversation" — 15 to 20 minutes, two or three offered slots. The Calmly outreach rule (26 August 2026): every first message states the **ultimate** ask. These are not in conflict once separated. The first email states in one sentence what the venture ultimately wants from this account (the ultimate ask, so the reader knows what the conversation is for), then asks for the conversation (the immediate ask, with slots). The ultimate ask is never softened into "explore" or "learn more"; the immediate ask is never inflated into a decision.

**Rules that apply to every email in the sequence:**

- **Short.** One thought per email. Kazanjy: people "want quick snippets of information." Target 120 words for the first email; never past 180.
- **Calm.** "Conversational, calm, text-based." No exclamation marks, no superlatives, no "bombastic claims." The house style already bans these; the sales voice is the Tom voice profile at its plainest.
- **Personalised by a person's judgement, not only by merge fields.** Kazanjy's figure: a personalised first email gets 12 per cent replies against 5 per cent for a merged one, and a sequence with a personalised first email gets 30 per cent more replies overall. Merge fields (name, company, number) are the floor. The research line is the personalisation.
- **One link, and it works.** A hosted page, a recorded walk-through or a screenshot made for them. No attachments.
- **Never "just following up."** Each later email carries a new thought (a second proof line, a cost figure, an answer to the likely objection). The sequence is written once, in full, before the first send.
- **The disclosure line is the whole signature.** "Finrad — the AI that runs [venture domain]." Nothing after it. (Tom, 11 September 2026.)
- **Timing.** Kazanjy: not Monday morning. Tuesday to Thursday, 07:00 or 10:00 local, or after 19:00. Reply-triggered sends go immediately (section 6).

## 5 · The social proof rule — the process point that started this document

**What the source says, and where it stops.** Kazanjy's evidence hierarchy (chapter 2) runs metric-based comparison first, third-party validation second (customer counts, testimonials with numbers, case studies, analyst coverage), qualitative claims last and only with a metric behind them. He also says founder status itself "reduce[s] the friction of getting these meetings." A mutual connection adds "a reputation layer." **He does not say what to put in the proof slot when the venture has no customers.** The rule below fills that gap. It is Tom's (15 September 2026), it is consistent with the source's own hierarchy, and it is the same move `landing-page-standard.md` §7.3 made for the landing page.

### 5.1 · The six levels of proof

Six kinds of proof, ranked. Use the **highest level that is true on the send date**. One level per email. A later email in the sequence may use the next level down as its new thought.

| Level | Kind | What it must contain | True when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Customer outcome | Named customer, the metric, the period | A paying customer has measured the outcome and agreed to be named |
| 2 | Customers in use | A count, or named logos, and what they use it for | Paying customers exist; the count is the exact count |
| 3 | Design partner or pilot | The partner's name or category, what is running, since when | A signed pilot exists; it is called a pilot, never a customer |
| 4 | Independent evidence | The published research, the standard, the regulator text or the independent review, cited | The source exists and says what the line claims (source-of-what check) |
| 5 | **Founder background** | See 5.2 | Always true; this is the launch default |
| 6 | Method provenance | The method the product is built on and who built it, cited | Always true; use only beneath level 5 or alongside it |

**Selection rule.** Levels 1 to 3 are empty for every venture at launch. In that state the proof line is level 5, founder background. Level 4 goes in the second email as its new thought. Level 6 is for the third or the break-up email. A venture does not skip level 5 for level 4 because level 4 sounds more objective. The reader of a cold email decides whether to trust the writer before they decide whether to trust a citation. The founder line answers the first question.

### 5.2 · The founder-background line — fields

The line has four fields and a length limit.

```
[Venture] was founded by [name], [the one prior role or fact most relevant to this prospect's problem],
[the scale or institution that makes it verifiable] — [one-click verification, optional].
```

- **Name** — the founder's, not the operator's. The operator is disclosed in the signature; the credibility is the founder's.
- **The relevant fact** — chosen for **relevance to the prospect's problem**, not for prestige. A bank proposition director selling to a bank leads with the £100m portfolio; the same founder selling to a marketplace leads with the consumer venture he co-founded. One fact. The second-most-relevant fact goes in a later email if at all.
- **The verifiable scale or institution** — a number or a named body the reader can check: a portfolio size, a named university lab, a named publication. No adjectives ("experienced", "senior", "award-winning").
- **Verification** — a link where one exists (a public profile, the lab page, the article). Where none exists, the fact must still be checkable by name.
- **Length:** 25 words or fewer. **Tone:** stated, not argued. It is a fact about who built the product, placed after what the product does.

**Three constraints from standing rulings, and one tension named.**

1. **Never a door-opener.** Tom's rule (2 September 2026): never use his name to open a door, and he spends no social capital on operational work. The founder line states a public fact about who built the product. It does not say "Tom would like to speak with you," does not offer Tom's time, and does not name any relationship Tom has with the reader or their organisation. Relationships (a warm introduction, a shared employer) are Tom's levers to pull, never the operator's to cite. *The 2 September rule reads as "keep Tom out of first contact"; this rule puts his background into it. The resolution: the earlier rule protects his time and his relationships, and a verifiable fact from a CV spends neither. Ruled by Tom, 15 September 2026: "CV facts are fine."*
2. **Only facts from the sent CVs.** The founder line is drawn from `05-Areas/Career/CVs/Tom_Manuel_CV_Master.pdf` and the sent variants, never from a newer draft (rule of 4 September 2026). The CV's own accuracy notes apply: "joint first," not "the first"; the consumer venture described by category, not brand; no journal named for the academic work.
3. **Nothing from the current employer.** No current-role fact appears in any venture's proof line while the Barclays confidentiality rule stands. Prior-role facts about the same institution are fine — they are on the sent CV.

### 5.3 · Worked lines

**Calmly Resolve, to a marketplace platform.** The site is the buyer. The problem is disputes that cost them the customer.

> Calmly was founded by Tom Manuel, who co-founded a consumer legal-tech venture and built the Built to Hold Corporate Innovation Lab at Cornell University.

Level 5. Relevant fact: the consumer legal-tech venture, because the reader's problem is a consumer dispute. Verifiable institution: Cornell. 24 words.

**Calmly Resolve, to a litigation funder.** The reader's question is whether the founder can model risk.

> Calmly was founded by Tom Manuel, a former Barclays Vice President responsible for £100m of income and a financial modeller for the IFC (World Bank Group).

Level 5. Relevant fact: the portfolio and the IFC modelling, because the reader prices risk. 25 words. Prior role, so the confidentiality rule does not apply.

**Forge, to an operator with an incumbent line:**

> Forge was built by Tom Manuel, who taught proposition methodology at Saïd Business School, Oxford, and built the Built to Hold Corporate Innovation Lab at Cornell.

Level 5, 25 words. The second email carries level 6: the method's provenance (Simanis, Cornell SC Johnson), cited.

**A level-4 second-email line for Calmly:**

> The mechanism is not ours: [author, year, journal] measured [the effect] in [population]. Independent review status: [state]. We are pre-launch and have no customer logos.

The last sentence is the landing-page rule (§7.3) applied to email: say the state plainly. An email that omits it invites the reader to assume the worst; one that states it reads as honest.

### 5.4 · Banned in the proof slot

- Any count that is not the exact count ("dozens", "a growing number", "leading companies").
- "Trusted by" with no named party behind it.
- A pilot or design partner described as a customer.
- A prospect who has replied described as a partner.
- A claim about the founder that the sent CV does not carry.
- A citation the writer has not opened (source-of-what rule, 9 September 2026).
- The founder's time or a relationship offered as the proof.

Every proof line is checked against the venture's claims register where one exists (Calmly's does) before the sequence is approved.

## 6 · The sequence — cadence adapted to an operator that does not phone

Kazanjy's pattern is about seven touches over about seven working days, alternating email and phone, ending with a "breaking up with you" email. His data: the second email gets the highest reply rate (18 per cent against 12 for the first), and rates hold near 17 per cent through the fifth. So a single email is a discarded list.

This studio's operator sends email and does not phone. The adaptation:

| Day | Touch | New thought it carries |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Email 1 | Research line · problem · what we do · founder line · two asks |
| 3 | Email 2 | The cost of the problem, in their numbers (narrative question 3) · level-4 proof |
| 5 | Email 3 | The likely objection, answered (from R4's want block) · the one link again |
| 8 | Email 4 | What has changed (narrative question 5) · level-6 provenance where relevant |
| 12 | Email 5 | The break-up: one sentence saying this is the last message, the ultimate ask restated, the link |

Five, not seven, because the phone touches are removed and not replaced. Adding emails in their place would breach the "one thought" rule — there are five thoughts in the narrative that a prospect needs before a conversation, and no more.

**Reply-triggered rules** (Kazanjy: act on engagement while it is warm):

- A reply of any kind stops the sequence and moves the record to "in conversation."
- An open or a click with no reply moves the next touch forward to the same day, if the mail tool reports it. If it does not, the calendar cadence stands.
- The first reply that asks a question the operator can answer from the record is answered by the operator, same day. The first reply that asks for a person, a term outside the envelope or a commitment goes to Tom the same day with a drafted response.

## 7 · The pipeline record

One record per account, kept in the venture's launch folder as a table or a sheet, no tool bought. Fields:

`account · person · role · demand indicator · level of proof used · sequence day · state (queued / in sequence / in conversation / pilot / customer / closed-no / closed-later) · next touch date · owner (operator / Tom)`

The weekly investor update reports four numbers from it: accounts in sequence, reply rate on email 1 and on the sequence, conversations held, and customers. The Part-1 line — 30 customers — is the number that ends this standard's applicability and starts Kazanjy's Part 2.

## 8 · Pitching and closing — the two chapters this standard does not yet cover

Kazanjy's chapters 7 (pitching) and 8 (closing) apply once a conversation is booked. They are not folded in here because the studio has not yet held one launch conversation to test them against. When the first is held, this section is written from what happened, against the source. Until then two rules from those chapters stand as placeholders: the pitch follows the seven-question narrative in order, and the close asks for the smallest commitment the fin-sim's first tranche needs, not the largest the product could justify.

## 9 · Checklist before any launch sequence sends

- [ ] The seven narrative questions are answered by citation to the design record, not written fresh
- [ ] The problem statement was said to one person in the industry and recognised
- [ ] Every account on the list has a recorded demand indicator
- [ ] Email 1 is five parts, one screen, 180 words or fewer, one link that resolves
- [ ] Both asks are present: the ultimate ask in one sentence, the conversation ask with slots
- [ ] The proof line is the highest true level on the send date; if levels 1 to 3 are empty it is the founder line
- [ ] The founder line has all four fields, 25 words or fewer, drawn from a sent CV, no current-employer fact, no relationship, no offer of Tom's time
- [ ] Every citation in the sequence has been opened and says what the line says
- [ ] The full five-email sequence is written before email 1 sends; no email says "following up"
- [ ] The signature is the disclosure line and nothing else
- [ ] The pipeline record exists with every field, and the reply-triggered rules are assigned to an owner
- [ ] House-style checker run on every email: `python3 .scripts/check-house-style.py <file>`
